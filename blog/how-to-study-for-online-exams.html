How to Study for Online Exams: A Complete Guide

Study guide · Updated September 2026 · ~11 min read

Online exams aren't just paper tests on a screen. The time pressure works differently, the question formats are different, and there's an entire category of things that can go wrong that never applied to a paper exam. Here's how to prepare for the format you're actually facing — the study methods with evidence behind them, and a test-day routine that removes the avoidable disasters.

What makes an online exam different

Before the study methods, it's worth being precise about what changes. Four things matter:

Time pressure is tighter per question

Online exams frequently allocate a fixed time per question, or a total time that assumes you don't linger. There's no flipping ahead to see what's coming and budgeting accordingly — especially when questions appear one at a time. The practical consequence is that hesitation costs more than it does on paper.

Questions often come from a randomized bank

Many instructors build a pool of questions and have the platform draw a random subset for each student, often with shuffled answer order. This has a direct study implication: you cannot predict which topics appear, so broad coverage beats deep focus on three sections you're betting on. It also means comparing answers with a classmate is meaningless, since you likely didn't get the same questions.

The format tests recognition and recall differently

Multiple choice tests recognition — can you identify the right answer among options. Fill-in-the-blank tests recall — can you produce it from nothing. These are genuinely different skills, and studying with methods that only train recognition leaves you exposed on the questions that demand production.

There are new ways to fail that have nothing to do with knowledge

Connection drops, a browser update mid-exam, an autosave that didn't complete, a dead laptop battery. None of these existed for a paper exam. Preparation for an online exam therefore includes preparing your setup, not just the material — which is the part students almost universally skip.

The study methods that actually work

Study techniques vary enormously in effectiveness, and the most popular ones are frequently the weakest. Here's what the evidence supports, in rough order of value.

1. Retrieval practice (the big one)

Close your notes and try to produce the answer from memory, then check. That act of retrieval — struggling to pull something out — is what strengthens the memory. Recognizing information on a page does almost nothing by comparison.

This is why re-reading is such a trap. It produces a strong feeling of familiarity that your brain misreads as knowledge. You've seen the material so often it feels known, right up until the exam asks you to produce it unaided. Retrieval practice feels harder and more uncomfortable, which is precisely the signal that it's working.

In practice: after reading a section, close it and write down everything you remember. Compare against the source. The gaps are your study list.

2. Spaced repetition

Six hours of study spread across six days beats six hours in one night, substantially. Each time you revisit material after partly forgetting it, the memory is rebuilt stronger. Cramming skips that mechanism entirely — which is why crammed material evaporates within days.

For a unit quiz, three or four short sessions across the preceding week is a realistic target. For a midterm, start one to two weeks out. If you use flashcards, an app with a built-in scheduling algorithm handles the spacing for you.

3. Practice in the exam's format

If the exam is multiple choice, practice with multiple choice. If it's fill-in-the-blank, practice producing terms cold. Training the wrong skill is a common and invisible failure — students who reviewed by flipping flashcards quickly often find they recognize every term and can produce none.

The best available practice material is old quizzes from the same course, taken under time pressure. If your instructor releases previous quizzes or a practice exam, that's the single highest-value resource you have.

4. Work problems, then check the reasoning

For anything quantitative or applied, working the problem yourself first is non-negotiable. Reading a worked solution produces the same false familiarity as re-reading — it all makes sense while you watch someone else do it.

Attempt it, then compare against the correct reasoning. Where the two diverge is the precise concept you're missing, and that's far more valuable information than the corrected answer. This is where an AI study tool that explains its reasoning earns its place — not for the answer, but for the explanation you check your own thinking against.

5. Explain it out loud

Try to explain a concept as if teaching someone with no background. The points where you stall, hedge, or reach for the textbook's exact phrasing are the points you don't actually understand. It's an uncomfortable and extremely efficient diagnostic.

What doesn't work (but feels like it does)

  • Highlighting. Nearly zero effect on retention. It feels productive because it's active, but you're sorting text, not learning it.
  • Re-reading. The most common study method and one of the least effective. Familiarity is not knowledge.
  • Copying notes out neatly. Transcription without processing. If rewriting helps you, rewrite them condensed and from memory — then it becomes retrieval practice.
  • Marathon sessions. Attention degrades badly after roughly an hour. Two focused hours beat five unfocused ones, and the extra three mostly produce guilt.
  • Studying with the group too early. Group study is excellent for testing each other after you know the material, and a well-disguised social event before that.

Preparing for open-book online exams

Open-book exams catch more students out than closed-book ones, because "open book" gets misheard as "no studying required."

Instructors writing an open-book exam know you have the materials. So they don't ask what a term means — they ask you to apply it, compare it, or work a problem with it. Lookup gets you almost nothing; understanding gets you everything.

The other trap is time. Searching unfamiliar notes under a countdown is brutally slow. Students who didn't study spend the exam scrolling and run out of clock.

Prepare by building a condensed reference sheet — one page of formulas, definitions, and the distinctions you keep confusing, organized so you can find anything in seconds. Making that sheet is the studying, and it's the version of open-book preparation that works.

The week before

  1. Find out what's actually on it. Check the syllabus and ask if it's unclear. Which chapters, which formats, how long, how many attempts, whether it's open book. Studying the wrong material is the most expensive possible mistake.
  2. Make a topic list and rate yourself. Every topic, marked confident / shaky / no idea. Study the bottom two categories. Most students do the reverse, because reviewing what you already know is pleasant.
  3. Do a timed practice run. Take an old quiz under exam conditions — timer on, notes closed, no interruptions. This reveals pacing problems while there's still time to fix them.
  4. Review your past mistakes. Every question you got wrong on earlier quizzes is a documented gap. If the same concept appears twice in that list, it will almost certainly appear again.
  5. Test your setup. Log into the platform, confirm your browser works, check your connection. Do this days early, not an hour before.

Test-day checklist

  • Wired or strong connection. Ethernet if you have it. If not, sit close to the router and ask housemates to avoid streaming.
  • Plugged in. Battery-dead-mid-exam is entirely avoidable and entirely devastating.
  • Notifications off. Do Not Disturb on both computer and phone. Close everything you don't need.
  • Read the front page. Time limit, number of attempts, and — critically — whether questions lock after answering. That last setting means no going back, and it changes your whole approach.
  • Have a backup plan. Know your instructor's email and your phone's hotspot before you need them.
  • Easy questions first. Unless questions lock. Points are points, and time spent stuck on question four while eight easy ones sit unanswered is the most common way to lose marks you knew.
  • Don't submit early. If there's time left, use it to check the questions you flagged. Nobody gets a bonus for finishing first.

If something goes wrong: screenshot the error, note the time, and email your instructor immediately — during the exam, not after. A message timestamped mid-attempt is far more credible than one sent later, and the platform's own attempt log will usually back you up.

After the exam

The twenty minutes most students skip. When grades are released, if your instructor allows review, go through every question you missed and write down what the correct reasoning was.

A wrong answer is a precise map of a gap that is still there — and on a cumulative final, it will be asked again. Students who review missed questions consistently outperform students who look at the score and close the tab, and it costs almost no time.

Frequently asked questions

How do you study for an online exam?

Use retrieval practice rather than re-reading: close your notes and try to produce the answer from memory, then check. Spread study across several sessions instead of one long one, practice in the same format the exam uses, and work old quizzes under time pressure. For open-book exams, build an organized reference sheet in advance.

Are online exams harder than in-person exams?

They're different rather than uniformly harder. Online exams tend to have tighter time limits per question and often draw from randomized question banks, which rewards broad coverage over deep focus on a few topics. They also add failure modes that don't exist on paper, like connection problems, so preparation includes checking your setup.

How do you study for an open-book online exam?

Prepare a condensed, organized reference — one page of formulas, definitions, and key distinctions, arranged so you can find anything in seconds. Open-book exams are usually written assuming you have the materials, so they test application and speed rather than recall. Students who don't study because it's open-book almost always run out of time.

How long before an exam should I start studying?

For a unit quiz, three to four short sessions across the preceding week beats one long session the night before. For a midterm or final, start one to two weeks out. The spacing matters more than the total hours — the same six hours spread over six days produces substantially better retention than six hours in one night.

What should I do if my internet cuts out during an online exam?

Reconnect and get back in as fast as you can, because most platforms run the timer on the server and it doesn't pause. Your saved answers are usually still there. Screenshot any error message, note the time, and email your instructor immediately — a message sent during the exam is far more credible than one sent afterwards.
