12 Best Chrome Extensions for College Students in 2026

Roundup · Updated September 2026 · ~10 min read

Your browser is where most of your coursework actually happens — readings, submissions, research, and every quiz. A handful of well-chosen extensions makes that environment meaningfully better; a pile of overlapping ones just makes Chrome slow. These are the twelve worth knowing about, grouped by the job they do, with an honest note on what each costs you.

Before you install anything

Extensions are small programs with real access to your browsing. Two minutes of caution saves a lot of trouble:

  • Check the permissions. "Read and change all your data on all websites" is a large amount of trust. Sometimes it's genuinely necessary — an ad blocker can't work otherwise — but it should match what the tool obviously needs.
  • Look at reviews and recency. A well-reviewed extension updated recently is far safer than one abandoned three years ago. Abandoned extensions get sold, and new owners sometimes change what they do.
  • Install from the Chrome Web Store. Not from a link in a forum post.
  • Prune periodically. Once a term, open your extensions page and remove anything you stopped using. This is the single highest-value security habit for a browser.

For focus

1. uBlock Origin

Best for: making pages readable and fast

The most efficient content blocker available, and a genuine productivity tool rather than just an ad blocker. Academic sites, journal portals, and news pages load dramatically faster without trackers, and long readings become far less exhausting without things sliding in from the edges. It's free, open source, and uses less memory than the alternatives.

The practical benefit for students is on slow campus Wi-Fi, where a page that loads in two seconds instead of eleven changes whether you actually do the reading.

2. LeechBlock NG

Best for: blocking the sites you keep opening by reflex

You set which sites to block and when. The value is in defeating the automatic behavior — opening a new tab and typing three letters of a social site before you've consciously decided to. LeechBlock intercepts that.

Set it to block during your scheduled study blocks rather than all day. Total bans tend to get disabled in frustration; scoped ones survive.

3. Dark Reader

Best for: reading at night without eye strain

Forces dark mode on sites that don't offer one, with per-site controls for contrast and brightness. For anyone doing serious reading after dark — which is most students — it's a comfort upgrade you notice immediately.

One caveat: it can mangle pages with heavy diagrams or color-coded charts. It's a per-site toggle, so disable it where the colors carry meaning.

For research and writing

4. Zotero Connector

Best for: citations you don't have to type

Zotero is free reference-management software; the Connector is the browser half. On a journal article, a book listing, or most web pages, one click saves the full citation with metadata. When you write, it generates the bibliography in whatever style your course requires.

For any course with a research paper, this saves several hours and eliminates the specific misery of reconstructing a source you read in week three. Save sources as you find them, not when you write — the whole point is that the work is already done.

5. Grammarly

Best for: catching what you stopped seeing

Checks grammar, spelling, and phrasing wherever you type, including Canvas text boxes and Google Docs. Its real value is as a second reader: after four passes on a draft, you read what you intended rather than what's there. A machine reader doesn't have that problem.

Take its mechanical corrections freely and its style suggestions skeptically — it optimizes for plain business prose, which isn't what every assignment wants.

6. Google Scholar Button

Best for: finding the actual paper

Highlight a title or citation, click the button, and it searches Google Scholar without leaving the page. Where your library has access, it often surfaces a PDF link directly. It removes the tab-juggling from chasing a reference, which is the friction that stops people following citations at all.

For coursework

7. Answerly AI

Best for: getting unstuck on Canvas questions

Built specifically for Canvas, which is what separates it from general AI tools. It runs on the quiz page itself, so there's no copying questions into another tab and no reformatting problems — it reads the question in place and gives you the answer with the reasoning behind it.

It handles multiple choice, matching, and fill-in-the-blank directly, and its screenshot tool covers anything it can't parse — diagrams, images, free-response prompts — by dragging a box around the question.

The reasoning is the part that matters for actually learning. An unexplained answer is unverifiable and teaches you nothing transferable; an explanation lets you compare against your own thinking and find the specific gap. Use it on practice quizzes and review, working the problem first, and it functions as a study tool rather than a shortcut. As always, your institution's academic integrity policy governs how you use any tool on graded work.

8. Wolfram Alpha (via bookmark or extension)

Best for: checking STEM work

Quick access to the computational engine that solves equations, converts units, and plots functions. With Pro it shows step-by-step working, which is where the learning value is — on most STEM exams partial credit comes from method, and knowing which line you always lose it on is worth more than any single corrected answer.

9. Toby or OneTab

Best for: the forty-tab research problem

Both solve the same problem differently. OneTab collapses every open tab into a single list, freeing memory instantly. Toby saves organized collections you can name and reopen — a "Bio 210 Final" set with your readings and notes, restored in one click.

Pick one. Running both is exactly the kind of overlap this list is trying to avoid.

For accessibility and reading

10. Read Aloud

Best for: long readings and proofreading

Reads page text aloud with adjustable voices and speed. Useful if you retain more by listening, if you have a reading-related disability, or for commutes. It's also an unexpectedly effective proofreading tool — hearing your own essay read back catches awkward sentences your eyes skip over.

11. Mercury Reader

Best for: stripping a page to just the article

Removes navigation, sidebars, and clutter, leaving clean text at a readable width. For badly designed course sites and journal pages with narrow columns, it turns an unpleasant reading experience into a normal one, and it prints far better than the original page.

12. Bitwarden

Best for: not reusing the same password everywhere

A free password manager. Students accumulate a startling number of logins — the LMS, the library, four textbook portals, a lab system — and the natural response is reusing one password, which means one breach compromises everything including your student account.

Bitwarden generates and fills unique passwords so you only remember one. It's free for personal use and takes twenty minutes to set up, and it's the item on this list most likely to save you from an actual disaster.

How to choose from this list

Twelve is a menu, not a shopping list. Installing all of them would slow your browser and give a dozen programs access to your browsing for no benefit. A realistic setup covers four jobs:

  • One blocker — uBlock Origin. Everyone benefits from this.
  • One writing aid — Grammarly, if your courses involve writing.
  • One coursework tool — matched to your actual courses: Zotero for research papers, Answerly AI for Canvas-heavy classes, Wolfram Alpha for STEM.
  • One security tool — Bitwarden. Not optional, really.

Add a focus blocker if you know you need one. Add tab management if you're a forty-tab person. Skip the rest until you feel the specific problem they solve.

A quick note on Edge, Brave, and Arc: all of these are Chromium-based and install Chrome Web Store extensions directly. Edge shows a one-time prompt to allow extensions from other stores; after that everything here works normally. Firefox and Safari have their own stores, and availability varies.

Frequently asked questions

What are the best Chrome extensions for college students?

The highest-value picks are uBlock Origin for cleaner pages, Zotero Connector for citations, Grammarly for writing, Answerly AI for Canvas quizzes, Dark Reader for late-night reading, and a blocker like LeechBlock for focus. Most students only need four or five, chosen to match the courses they're actually taking.

Are Chrome extensions safe to install?

Most are, but permissions matter. An extension that asks to read and change data on all websites can see everything you browse, so install only from the Chrome Web Store, check the review count and recency, read the privacy policy, and remove anything you stopped using. Extensions that change ownership are a known risk, which is why periodic cleanup is worth doing.

Do Chrome extensions work on Microsoft Edge?

Yes. Edge is built on Chromium and can install extensions directly from the Chrome Web Store — you may see a one-time prompt allowing extensions from other stores. Brave, Opera, Vivaldi, and Arc are also Chromium-based and generally work the same way.

How many extensions should I install?

Fewer than you think. Each one uses memory and can slow browsing, and each carries its own permissions. Four to six well-chosen extensions covering distinct jobs is plenty; long lists of overlapping tools mostly cost performance.

Is there a Chrome extension that helps with Canvas quizzes?

Answerly AI runs directly inside Canvas and handles multiple choice, matching, and fill-in-the-blank questions, with a screenshot tool for diagrams and free-response items. It shows the reasoning behind each answer rather than just the answer, which is what makes it useful for review and practice rather than only for speed.
